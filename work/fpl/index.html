How it works

The system is predict-and-optimise. A LightGBM point predictor (one booster per position, 122 features) estimates each player's expected points for the coming gameweek from ten seasons of history. That history comes from the community's season archives, Understat expected goals and assists, prior-season stats from FBref and FotMob, Pinnacle match odds, the game's own expected-points field, the new defensive-contribution counts, and since August the bookmakers' player-prop odds.

A mixed-integer programme (PuLP with the CBC solver) then picks the squad, the transfers under the real bank, selling-price and free-transfer rules, the starting eleven, the captain, and a bench order weighted by auto-substitution probabilities.

Why an engine. The rules are the hard part: eight legal formations, chips with half-season expiry, free transfers banking to five, selling price equal to purchase price plus half the appreciation, hits of four points, automatic substitutions with captain failover, double and blank gameweeks. I put all of them in one stateless engine, covered by 368 tests, and the optimiser, the backtests and the live season all call it.

The project started as reinforcement learning, and the environment still exists. It has an 18-dimensional action space (five transfer pairs, lineup, captain and chip) and a 1,363-dimensional observation, and the agent is MaskablePPO, run on a 40-CPU cluster node. The RL agent has no validated result against the optimiser on held-out seasons, so despite the repository's name it stays a research path and the optimiser is what runs. The repository's own rule is to retrain the agent only if it beats the optimiser on holdout.

Backtests

I ran two chip-free season replays, each with the predictor trained only on earlier seasons. On 2024/25, using the April scripts and the 86-feature model of that time, the pipeline scores 2,918 net points with at most one transfer per gameweek (37 transfers, no hits) and 3,171 with up to five transfers per gameweek. A no-transfer squad scores 1,950 and a perfect-foresight oracle 3,713. For scale, the highest total a human manager has ever recorded is 2,844, in 2021/22 (Premier League), with chips.

For 2025/26, the first season under the new defensive-contribution scoring, I wrote a separate harness in August. It retrains its own evaluation model on the seasons before the target and replays the year through the optimiser and the full rules engine. It scores 2,261 at one transfer per gameweek and 2,566 unconstrained (122 transfers, 336 points of hits). Both are below the all-time record, and neither is comparable with a real season played with chips. The two seasons didn't go through the same harness, so their levels aren't directly comparable with each other either.

I measured where the gap between the two seasons comes from. The predictor's per-gameweek rank correlation with actual points is 0.75 to 0.79 on every expanding-window fold from 2021/22 to 2025/26. Its error on "haulers" (players scoring five or more), though, jumps from about 3.6 on the four earlier seasons to 5.84 on 2025/26, because the scoring regime changed. Training inside the new era brings it back to 5.23. As far as I can tell, the rest of the 2025/26 gap is that the new scoring rewards a kind of player the historical features don't describe.

Against other predictors

The only clean external comparison scores both models on exactly the same player-gameweek rows. Against theFPLkiwi's public projections, the predictor's per-gameweek rank correlation is 0.756 against 0.694 on 2021/22 (38 gameweeks, 19,603 rows) and 0.798 against 0.701 on 2022/23 (27 gameweeks), with lower error in three of the four outcome bins. theFPLkiwi is better on the three-to-four-point "tickers" bin in both seasons. Under the protocol published with OpenFPL, the predictor trains through 2023/24 and is evaluated on 2024/25 gameweeks 32 to 38. On that protocol its conditional error is lower than the numbers reported in that paper for OpenFPL and for a commercial service in three of four bins, and higher in one. Those competitor figures are quoted from arXiv 2508.09992, not re-run, so I treat that comparison as indicative.

Experiments that didn't make it

  • Balanced sample weights were meant to help with haulers. They cut the per-gameweek correlation from 0.747 to 0.662.
  • A Tweedie loss improved rank correlation by 0.0016 and the zero and blank bins, but it made overall error worse, along with the ticker and hauler bins, where the errors are largest. I rejected it.
  • Defensive-contribution features were neutral (rank correlation moved by 0.0005 and error by 0.007 the other way, both within noise). I kept them.
  • Head-to-head match odds added nothing.
  • Player-prop odds were the one feature win of the summer. They gave −2.5% error and a higher correlation in all eight held-out gameweeks, a small effect that survived the sign test.
  • The expected-points "leak". An April report claimed the game's own expected-points field leaked post-match information. Capturing the field mid-gameweek and reproducing its formula to 99.3% showed it doesn't, and it's still the most important feature.

Known gaps

  • The optimiser only plans one gameweek at a time. There's no chip scheduling, no value on banking a free transfer and no fixture-swing planning. This is the largest missing piece.
  • There's no expected-minutes model and no bonus-point model.
  • The live entry is one season of one team. The tracker is a diary, and it doesn't validate anything.
