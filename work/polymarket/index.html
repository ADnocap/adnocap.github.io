The market

Polymarket lists a binary market every fifteen minutes for each of BTC, ETH, SOL and XRP: will the price be up or down at settlement? Each contract pays $1 or $0. The order books for Up and for Down are separate, so the two asks need not sum to one, and the 15-minute cadence means a strategy settles a few hundred times a day. That volume is the attraction: hypotheses get answered in days rather than quarters.

The edge we spent most of our time on is the oldest one in binary markets. Ten minutes before settlement, across 10,113 collected markets, the side priced near 0.80 wins about 90% of the time and the side priced near 0.20 wins about 8%: favourites are underpriced and longshots overpriced. Figure 1 is that calibration curve. Whether the mispricing survives a 0.65 to 0.95 entry, exchange fees and the queue is the question the rest of the page answers.

The strategies

Six crypto Up/Down strategy families were researched. S5 and S6 have live records worth reading; S4 and S8 traded briefly; S1 and S7 never traded. Backtest numbers are labelled as such. The Sharpe figures on this page are not computed on one footing, so do not compare them with each other or read them as fund-style numbers: the S7 pair (9.5 and 0.85) is annualised from daily-summed backtest P&L, counting only days on which a trade settled; the S4 figure (2.2) is annualised from weekly portfolio returns over about 31 weeks with open positions valued at cost; the sizing-study figures further down are per-trade ratios on binary payoffs.

  1. S5Favourite press traded live
    Idea
    Buy the higher-priced side of a 15-minute market 5 to 15 minutes before settlement, when its ask is between 0.65 and 0.95 and the two asks sum to less than 1.15; hold to settlement.
    Evidence
    Live, 10 to 28 April: 3,037 trades, 78.0% win rate, +$383 on-chain verified, +0.80% per rolled dollar.
    Outcome
    Traded live; the only strategy with a verified live profit.
  2. S5On 5-minute markets killed live
    Idea
    Same rule on the 5-minute cadence.
    Evidence
    Live: 216 trades, 71.3% win rate, −$494, despite positive backtests.
    Outcome
    Disabled. Postmortem 2.
  3. S6Machine-learned gate flat live
    Idea
    Trade S5 signals only when a LightGBM model's expected value clears 5%: 102 features from the order book, its trajectory, Binance candles and on-chain flow.
    Evidence
    Walk-forward backtest 81.0% win rate; live on 5-minute markets: 288 trades, +$4.61.
    Outcome
    Flat live; repurposed to 15-minute markets, XRP dropped for miscalibration.
  4. S1Streak reversal researched
    Idea
    After three to five same-direction outcomes in a row, the next 5-minute market reverses 53 to 57% of the time while opening prices stay near 50/50. Post-only maker orders.
    Evidence
    Out-of-sample backtest: 2,745 trades, 53.2% win rate, +$64; the same trades executed as taker lose $95.
    Outcome
    Researched; the edge is smaller than the taker fee, so maker-only or nothing.
  5. S4Model-priced daily markets backtest
    Idea
    Price 4- to 30-day above/below markets with the Phantom forecaster's Student-t distribution and buy the side with edge.
    Evidence
    Backtest only, August 2025 to March 2026: 1,118 trades, 67% win rate, weekly Sharpe 2.2, maximum drawdown −36% with delta-aware sizing.
    Outcome
    21 live executions in April; no live result worth reporting.
  6. S7At-the-money strike leak, never traded
    Idea
    Hourly "above $X" markets, entered 15 minutes before settlement at the strike nearest the spot price.
    Evidence
    Backtest Sharpe 9.5 with the leak, 0.85 without it.
    Outcome
    Never traded. Postmortem 1.
  7. S84-hour favourite press added April
    Idea
    Favourite press on 4-hour markets with an order-book-imbalance filter (my collaborator's research).
    Evidence
    Backtest, October 2025 to April 2026: 754 trades, 91.8% win rate, +7.8% return on stake, in-sample and out-of-sample splits agree.
    Outcome
    Added in April; no separate live tally survives.

The system

The first generation, written in December and January, was a monolith: a cross-venue matcher (sentence embeddings and a language model to pair equivalent markets across exchanges), a Deribit-priced fair-value engine for daily BTC and ETH markets, and an executor that could place live orders from 23 January. It was retired in March for a hub-and-worker design that we ran until June.

Data engineering. The tick-level research used the pmxt Polymarket order-book archive (CC BY 4.0): hourly Parquet files of 500 to 900 MB, about 37 million rows each. Six DuckDB workers read them over HTTP with predicate pushdown on the market IDs we needed, after resolving those IDs through the exchange's metadata API at about 200 requests per second. The February to April extract was estimated at 50 to 80 GB.
  • Hub. A FastAPI process that owns the strategy registry, the state aggregator, a WebSocket that broadcasts state to the dashboard once a second, and an IPC server. Persistence is SQLite in write-ahead mode: positions, executions, wallet snapshots, an activity log.
  • Workers. One subprocess per strategy, talking to the hub over newline-delimited JSON on a local TCP socket, with a five-second heartbeat, a fifteen-second timeout and exponential-backoff restarts. A strategy that crashes restarts alone; the others keep trading.
  • Dashboard. React and TypeScript, served behind Caddy: P&L, win rate and inventory by strategy, a discovery view and the logs. About 6,700 lines.
  • Research pipeline. Encoded as Claude Code skills, including an eight-step research procedure with two hard gates: no backtest before a statistical validation has passed, no capacity estimate before robustness checks have. Execution rules were written down once and enforced everywhere: buying means paying the ask; the Up and Down books are independent; backtests never assume limit-order fills.

The production tree was about 20,000 lines of Python and 6,700 of TypeScript, inside a repository of 505 commits, on a small cloud server. I authored the large majority of it.

What happened live

The hub went live on 28 March with three strategies on a different market type (negative-risk multi-outcome markets): a dispersion trade, an Avellaneda-Stoikov market maker with a regime switch on order-flow toxicity, and a tail-theta position. In eleven days the market maker lost $168.64 and the others roughly broke even; all three were retired on 9 April when S5 and S6 replaced them.

S5 traded from 9 April. An execution audit over its first eleven days (2,527 intended buys, 95.4% filled, median signal-to-fill latency 4.31 seconds) found that price slippage was slightly favourable and that the realised win rate, 78.2% with a 95% interval of 76.5 to 79.8%, was significantly above the backtest's 75.6% (binomial z = 3.02). The implementation shortfall came from failed and partial fills, not from prices. Figure 3 shows those days.

Then the second half of April happened. The dashboard's cumulative curve (Figure 4) peaked near +$800 and gave back a third of it, almost all of it on ETH, which lost $194 on the dashboard ($181 after reconciliation) while XRP made $466 and SOL $261.

The reconciliation in May found something else: the dashboard had been counting partial fills that never completed. Against the on-chain record, S5's result from 10 to 28 April was +$383 on 3,037 trades, a 78.0% win rate and +0.80% per dollar rolled, not the +$549 on screen. Slippage over all 3,037 trades summed to $3.

By May the exchange had migrated its order API and we spent the last engineering commits on fee accounting. The tuning memo written at that point recommended dropping ETH, tightening the sum-of-asks filter to 1.02 and trading only the 10 to 15 minute entry window, and prescribed a two-week verification period before scaling. We did not run that verification: the MSc, the internship and the research were taking the time, and the honest reading of the numbers was a small, real, fragile edge on a small wallet. We stopped.

Postmortem 1: fifteen minutes of look-ahead

In April we researched an hourly strategy: at fifteen minutes before settlement, find the "above $X" market whose strike sits nearest the spot price and trade its favourite. The first backtest was spectacular, 98.8% win rate, +8.3% return on stake and an annualised Sharpe of 9.5 on daily backtest P&L, and that is exactly what made us look again.

The bug was one function argument. The helper that fetched the spot price to choose the strike was called with the settlement time rather than the entry time, so the backtest picked its strike knowing where the price would end up. Fifteen minutes of drift in BTC is enough to turn a coin flip into a near-certainty.

With the entry-time price the strategy still wins 94.5% of its trades, because favourites near settlement usually do, but the losses are about fifteen times the size of the wins: over 696 trades the corrected backtest makes $85 at 25 contracts a trade, a 0.52% return on stake, an annualised Sharpe of 0.85 on daily P&L and a $155 drawdown. What changed afterwards: the S7 backtest was rewritten to read the spot price at the entry timestamp, and the postmortem in the repository overview records the rule in writing: verify that a backtest uses only information available at decision time. The research procedure written that week already required every signal to be computed only from data available at time T, and it carries a no-look-ahead item on its go-live checklist. The general lesson is older than us: when a backtest looks like a money printer, the first hypothesis is that it is reading the answer key.

Postmortem 2: a live loss that killed a variant

S5 traded both the 15-minute and the 5-minute cadence from its first live day; the backtests were positive on both, with the 5-minute edge the thinner of the two. Live, the 5-minute variant won 71.3% of 216 trades and lost $494. The two facts are compatible: at the 0.65 to 0.95 entries the strategy takes, a 71% win rate is below break-even for anything bought above 0.71.

The repository's diagnosis was price-band specific rather than an execution problem: the 0.68 to 0.83 entry band that backtested well on 5-minute markets ran below break-even live, and the fills themselves were clean (orders filled at the quoted ask with median slippage of zero), so the backtest, not the execution, was wrong.

Rather than tune it, we cut the 5-minute markets entirely, raised the minimum entry price from 0.55 to 0.65, removed a "dead zone" exclusion the backtest had suggested and reduced the bet size from 25 to 10 contracts while the 15-minute variant proved itself. The machine-learned gate, which had been flat on the 5-minute markets (288 trades, +$4.61), was moved to the 15-minute cadence for the same reason.

The rule we kept: a live result that diverges from its backtest is a data point about the backtest, and the response is to stop, not to average it in.

Smaller things we learned

  • Execution is the strategy. Polymarket charged a 7.2% taker fee on these crypto markets. Every configuration of the streak-reversal strategy is profitable as a maker and loses as a taker on the same trades; Figure 10 is the whole argument.
  • Sizing is not edge. In a walk-forward study of the favourite-press gates (8,726 markets, 60/40 split), half-Kelly sizing multiplied both profit and drawdown by 2.5 and left the per-trade Sharpe unchanged at 0.38, while rule-based size tilts made it worse (0.34). The production filter alone lifted it from 0.27 to 0.38; a logistic expected-value gate reached 0.41 with a third fewer trades.
  • The edge sat in one bucket. In the 18-day live sample, trades where the two asks summed to between 1.00 and 1.02 made $521 (n = 2,070, z = 2.39); every wider-spread bucket lost. Entries 10 to 15 minutes before settlement made money; entries 5 to 10 minutes before did not.
  • Within-window moves matter in a barbell. Over 2,346 paper trades, the favourite's edge over break-even was +3.0 points when the underlying had barely moved since the window opened, +0.1 when it had moved 5 to 10 basis points, and +5.9 when it had moved more than 20.
  • A fair value from options was not a better price. A June research memo priced daily and 4-hour markets off Deribit at-the-money implied volatility (a no-smile model built on sparse, 15-minute trade-print anchors), with pre-registered gates, over 1,304 markets. On daily "above" ladders the model was statistically indistinguishable from the market mid (Brier difference −0.0012, 95% interval including zero); on 4-hour markets the market was clearly better. The memo's one positive, a NO-side edge on entries taken when the model's fair value sat more than 2 or 3 cents from the executable price, had n = 71 to 108 and was flagged by the memo itself as a launch-window effect, expected to decay as other quoters arrived (the series was three weeks old).

Afterwards: the June rewrite

In June I rebuilt the trading infrastructure from scratch, alone, as a production-only system: no research code, no old strategies ported. The design goal was that the system should be unable to do the things that had cost us money or sleep. Git shows 64 commits and 22,770 lines of Python in the first 53 hours, and 77 commits, 24,076 lines of Python (8,599 of them tests, 381 test functions) after about 100 hours across five days. Every commit carries a Claude Code co-author trailer; the pace is the tooling's, the architecture is mine.

  • One chokepoint. Strategies are separate operating-system processes that never import the exchange client, never hold a key and never open a socket. They publish order intents to a durable Redis stream; a single order-gateway process consumes them, runs the risk checks, signs, posts, persists to Postgres and only then acknowledges the stream entry, so a crash replays rather than loses.
  • Eleven ordered pre-trade checks. Kill switch and trading-enabled flag, connectivity, market status, allow-list, order validity, price collar, per-strategy limits, firm-wide limits, daily loss limits, rate guard, self-trade prevention. Any exception inside a check rejects the order: the pipeline fails closed.
  • Four independent gates. The strategy's service unit, a quoting flag in its config, the gateway's dry-run default and a trading-enabled flag in Redis must all be opened, separately and deliberately, before an order can reach the venue. The dashboard is read-only by design; halting is a control-plane action.
  • Live and simulated share one interface. The backtest engine replays recorded market data through the same strategy context as production. One bug found this way: cancels silently failed in simulation, so any quote-and-requote strategy would have shown "fantasy" maker fills. Fixed with a regression test on 16 June.

The first strategy was a two-sided maker on the noon daily BTC and ETH markets. The research behind it found that a fair value from the Deribit volatility surface beats the market's own Brier score (0.0631 versus 0.0643 on 2.9 million observations across 7,402 markets) and also that centring quotes on that fair value hurts in every simulated configuration, so the maker quotes around the book mid and the model stays out of it entirely; risk is handled by inventory caps. It went live at ten shares a quote with a $50 daily loss cap.

Three pilot days, three lessons:

  • 15 June. The maker re-quoted at about 290 orders a minute with zero fills and built a backlog the gateway could not drain. Fixed with an absolute re-quote threshold and one order per leg; order rate fell to about three a minute.
  • 16 June. It accumulated one leg in a trend. Fixed with an inventory-reduction path and a cost-to-complete gate that, replayed over that day's 7.2 million recorded events, moved the mark-to-market from −$3.51 to +$10.81 in simulation.
  • 17 June. It lost $9.24 to sub-tick order rejects near settlement and to quoting markets that were already decided. Fixed by clamping the tick grid and narrowing the quote band to 0.20 to 0.80.

Then I halted it. The simulation's six-month estimate depends on an unobservable queue-position parameter and I do not quote it.

What I would do differently

  • Keep one consolidated ledger from the first trade. The whole-run P&L of the system is reconstructable only from the exchange, because research databases, dashboard tables and audit scripts each covered part of the period.
  • Reconcile against on-chain fills daily rather than at month end. The phantom-fill bug hid for three weeks.
  • Start with the fail-closed gateway. The June architecture is what the March system should have been; the March system was built to answer a research question fast, and it did.
  • Decide the stop rule in writing before going live. We stopped for good reasons, but the tuning memo's two-week verification was the right experiment and it never ran.
